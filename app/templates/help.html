How to use the Plasmid Editor

Everything here works on a sequence: your own, one from the MoClo library, or one pasted in. This page covers getting a sequence in, editing and annotating it, restriction analysis, the cloning simulations, primers and PCR, judging sequencing reads, and what to do when something looks wrong. Golden rules

  1. Save a sequence before you work on it. An unsaved sequence is yours alone and goes when the tab does. Save as… puts it in My sequences, where the rest of the suite can reach it.
  2. Annotate once, properly. A plasmid with its promoter, CDS and terminator marked explains itself to everyone later; one with a bare sequence explains nothing.
  3. Simulate before you clone. A digest, a PCR or an assembly takes a minute here and saves a failed week at the bench.
  4. Read the whole product, not just its size. A simulated product of the right length can still have the wrong insert in it.
  5. Confirm a clone from its reads, not from a gel alone. Confirming makes the glycerol stock in Lab Stocks, so the tube in the freezer is tied to the sequence that proved it.
  6. Keep one plasmid, not five copies. Edit the saved one; its history keeps what it was.

Getting a sequence in

  • Open a file: GenBank, FASTA, SnapGene (.dna) or plain sequence. Features and colours come with it.
  • Paste bases straight into an empty editor.
  • My sequences lists what you and the lab have saved.
  • MoClo parts, backbones and assembled plasmids open here directly, since both tools share the same library.
  • New starts an empty sequence to type or paste into.

Mark a plasmid circular if it is: it changes restriction mapping, PCR across the origin, and how reads are aligned.

Editing

Click in the sequence and type: A, C, G and T insert, Backspace and Delete remove, and the arrow keys, Home, End and shift-select work as in a text editor. Ctrl+Z undoes.

  • Copy, Copy RC (reverse complement) and Copy AA (translated) for the selection.
  • Rev-comp flips the whole sequence, Topology switches circular and linear, and Set origin makes the selected base number 1.
  • Translate adds the protein of the selection, in the reading frame you choose.
  • Sequence and Map switch between the bases and the circular or linear map.

Every save keeps the version before it, so editing a saved plasmid is safe: History shows what it was.

Features and annotation

  1. Select the bases and press Add feature: a name, a type (promoter, CDS, terminator…), the strand and a colour.
  2. Auto-annotate marks what it recognises from a feature library, which is the quick way to make sense of a sequence from elsewhere.
  3. The Features tab holds the libraries. The lab's own library can be edited there, and ApE's tab-separated format is read and written unchanged, so libraries move between the two programs.

Colours follow ApE's GenBank conventions, so a file annotated here opens in ApE with its colours, and the other way round.

Enzymes and digests

  • The Enzymes panel lists what cuts the open sequence and where. Filter by name, show only enzymes cutting a chosen number of times, or show the ones that do not cut at all — which is how you find a site to linearise with.
  • Choose… picks which set of enzymes to consider; the lab's own lists can be saved.
  • Digest / gel cuts with the enzymes you pick and draws the fragments as a virtual gel, so you know what the real gel should look like before you run it.
  • Silent sites finds where a site could be added or removed without changing the protein.

Cloning simulations

The Cloning tab simulates an assembly and gives you the product to save and annotate:

  • Golden Gate — one-pot Type IIS assembly. Pick the parts and the enzyme; it keeps the fragments that no longer carry a site and joins them by their overhangs, and reports per part what it used, which is how you find the part that is stopping an assembly.
  • Gibson / In-Fusion / HiFi — joins fragments in the order given, through terminal homology. It flips a fragment automatically where only its reverse complement overlaps, and reports each junction with its overlap and melting temperature.
  • Restriction and ligation — cut a vector and an insert with chosen enzymes, pick the fragment of each, and see the ligation products.
  • Recombination — see below.

Gateway, Cre-lox and FRT

Recombination covers a double crossover through two shared core sequences: Gateway LR (attL × attR) and BP (attB × attP), and loxP or FRT exchange.

  1. Pick the donor first (the entry clone, or the attB PCR product), then the acceptor (the destination vector, or pDONR).
  2. Leave the minimum core at 20 bp. Real cores are larger — att cores are 21–25 bp, loxP and FRT 34 bp — and a shorter setting starts matching by chance.
  3. Both products of the crossover are listed, as the reaction really gives: in an LR, the expression clone first, then the by-product carrying ccdB and the entry backbone. Each says how many bases came from the donor and how many from the acceptor.
  4. Save the one you want. It keeps the features of both parents: your insert, and the backbone's marker and origin.

It does not matter where the entry clone's file happens to start: a circular donor is read round its origin, so you get the same answer either way.

Primers and PCR

  • Design primers for the selected region, to a melting temperature you choose, with tails added for the cloning method you are using.
  • Simulate PCR with a forward and reverse primer, allowing mismatches, to see the product and its size before ordering anything.
  • Lab primers shows which of the lab's primers, from Lab Stocks, bind the open plasmid and where — so a sequencing primer you already own is found before a new one is ordered.
  • A designed primer can be saved straight to Lab Stocks, keeping the sequence exactly as designed.

Sequencing reads

  1. Sequencing tab: choose the plasmid the reads are of.
  2. Upload the reads — .ab1 chromatograms or FASTA — or paste them.
  3. Each read is aligned and shown against the reference, with its chromatogram, its quality, and every mismatch marked. The coverage across the plasmid shows what the reads reached and what they did not.
  4. Give each read its clone number as you upload it. That is what lets the reads of one colony be compared with each other, below.
  5. Judge each read: whether it is good enough, and what it says about the clone.

Comparing the reads of a clone

A single read cannot tell a real mutation from a bad basecall. Sanger reads are ragged at both ends, and one wrong base there looks exactly like a point mutation. Two reads that cover the same base can tell them apart, so Clone by clone groups the reads by the clone number you gave them and judges every difference by the reads that actually reach it:

  • Confirmed — every read covering that base shows the difference, and at least two do. This is the clone's own sequence.
  • Contradicted — other reads covering the base show the reference instead, so one of them misread. It is set aside, unless the reads showing it outnumber those that do not.
  • Only one read — nothing else reaches that base. It still counts as a real difference, because missing a genuine mutation is the worse mistake, but it is marked as resting on that one read: sequence the other strand before trusting it.

Each clone shows how much of the plasmid its reads reached between them, and reads as clean where covered when every base they reach matches. “Clean” is only ever a statement about the covered part — a clone read over 40% of its length says nothing about the other 60%. Click any difference to open a read that shows it, at that base.

The comparison needs the clone number: two reads with none are not treated as evidence about each other, since nothing says they came from the same colony.

Putting reads into a clone

Three ways, whichever suits:

  • As you upload. Type the clone into Clone number before pressing Add reads, and every read in that batch gets it. The box keeps what you typed, so the usual run is: type 1, add that colony's reads; change it to 2, add the next.
  • Several at once, afterwards. Tick the reads in the map, then type the clone in the bar that appears above it and press Put in this clone. Useful when reads arrive from the sequencing service in one batch with nothing to say which colony each came from.
  • One at a time. Open a read and type in its Clone box; it saves when you press Enter or click away.

Changing a clone number never changes a verdict. Saying which colony a read came from is not a judgement about the read, so a read already judged sufficient stays judged, by whoever judged it. Leave the box empty to take a read out of a clone again.

Calling a base yourself

A basecaller commits to a letter even where the peak is obscured, and it is often wrong near the ends of a read or under a dye blob. If you look at the chromatogram and judge that the trace says something else, you can say so.

  1. Open the read and click the base in the alignment. The chromatogram scrolls to it.
  2. Under the trace it shows which base of the read this is, what the basecaller called and its quality, and what the reference has there.
  3. Press A, C, G, T or N for what you read the trace as.

The read is aligned again straight away, so a base called back to the reference stops being a mismatch — in the read's differences, in the coverage and in the clone comparison. Calling a base the basecaller read as the reference works the same way and creates a difference, which is how you record a real mutation it missed.

Nothing is overwritten. The read keeps the bases exactly as the instrument gave them; your call is laid over the top, with the letter the machine called, its quality, your name and the time. Undo my call puts the instrument's letter back and aligns again. This is also why a hand call survives Re-align all.

Calling a base changes the read, not the plasmid. To change the plasmid to what a read shows — the other direction — use Correct this one or Correct all to this read above the alignment, which saves a new version of the sequence.

How the alignment works

Every read goes through the same four steps.

  1. Trimming. A chromatogram's ragged ends are cut off first, Mott-style: the longest stretch whose mean quality over a 10-base window stays at Q20 or better (about one expected error in 100 bases) is kept, and the rest discarded. FASTA reads carry no quality, so they are aligned whole. A read with fewer than 20 bases left is reported as too short rather than forced into an alignment.
  2. Both strands. The read and its reverse complement are each aligned, and whichever scores better is kept, so it does not matter which primer the read came from.
  3. The alignment itself. A local alignment with affine gap costs, computed by Biopython's PairwiseAligner, which uses Gotoh's algorithm for affine gaps — the local case being the Smith–Waterman family. Local, not global, because a read covers part of a plasmid and should align over that part without being punished for the rest. Affine, because one long gap is far likelier than many short ones, so opening a gap is charged heavily and continuing it cheaply. The scores are match +2, mismatch −3, gap open −5, gap extend −2.
  4. Differences are then read off the aligned columns in reference coordinates, so they match the positions shown everywhere else. A run of inserted or deleted bases is held as one difference with its length, not one per base, and each carries the quality of the read bases behind it — the worst of them, for a run — which is what the clone comparison weighs when two reads disagree.

Circular plasmids

A read that runs off the end of a circular plasmid and back to the start would otherwise align as two broken pieces. The reference is temporarily extended past its own end before aligning (by twice the read's length, or the whole plasmid, whichever is less), so such a read aligns in one piece; its coordinates are then wrapped back into the plasmid.

Large insertions

A local alignment stops at the first gap too expensive to cross, which silently drops whichever side of a large insertion scores less. So after the main alignment, each leftover end of the read is aligned again on its own. A leftover piece is accepted as a genuine second part of the read only if it is at least 25 bases long and at least 80% identical, and only if it reaches reference the main alignment did not already cover; below that, contamination or adapter read-through starts clearing the bar by chance. The pieces are then stitched into one alignment, with the insertion between them reported as a single difference.

When a read counts as mapped

A read is called mapped when at least 20 bases aligned and at least 80% of the bases that paired up match. A read failing that is shown as “did not align” rather than being drawn against a reference it does not belong to — usually the wrong plasmid, the wrong tube, or a failed reaction.

Two identity figures are reported, because one number cannot say both things. Base identity counts only the positions where a base pairs with a base, which is what decides whether the read is this plasmid at all — a read with a clean 60 bp insertion is still the right plasmid. Identity counts every column, each inserted or deleted base against it, so that same read does not report as 100%.

Elsewhere in the editor

The same aligner does the pairwise comparison of two sequences, in global mode with free end gaps (semi-global), so one sequence may be shorter without its overhanging ends being charged for. Protein comparisons use BLOSUM62 with gap costs of −10 to open and −0.5 to extend.

Confirming a clone

When the reads say the clone is right, confirm the plasmid and name the clone you confirmed. Two things follow:

  • The plasmid is marked confirmed, with who confirmed it and from which reads.
  • Its glycerol stock appears in Lab Stocks with the next code, already linked to this plasmid. Fill in the strain and where the tube is, and print its label.

This is the step that ties the tube in the freezer to the sequence that proved it, so do it here rather than entering the stock by hand.

More than one glycerol stock

A plasmid is usually kept as more than one stock: the cloning strain it was built in, and the strain it was moved into for the experiment — E. coli and Agrobacterium, say. Confirming a plasmid names one of them. The rest are added afterwards.

Under Verification — on any plasmid, confirmed or not — every glycerol stock Lab Stocks has for this plasmid is listed with its strain and where the tube is kept. Add a stock offers the same two choices as confirming:

  • A new glycerol stock, which Lab Stocks numbers with the lab's next code. Give the strain and where it is kept.
  • One that already has a number — a tube already in Lab Stocks, perhaps brought over from eLab. Giving its number links it to this plasmid so it is listed here too.

Nothing is renumbered and nothing is replaced: the number recorded when the plasmid was confirmed stays as it is, and each stock keeps its own code and label. A number Lab Stocks does not have is refused rather than quietly recorded, so add it there first.

A stock only appears in this list once it names the plasmid itself rather than just its name, which is why one brought over from eLab may need adding here once.

MoClo and the rest of the suite

  • Tick a plasmid Golden Gate ready and the editor checks that one Type IIS enzyme releases a single clean insert with two 4 bp fusion sites, then offers to add it to the shared MoClo parts library. If it cannot, it says why.
  • MoClo's parts, backbones and assembled plasmids open here for editing or read alignment.
  • Notebook: type @ in a page to link a plasmid, and it opens here.
  • Seed Bank: from a plasmid you can reach the lines made with it, their seeds and plants.
  • A plasmid marked public is readable by every lab in the suite; otherwise it stays with your own labs.

Fixing mistakes

  • An assembly gives no product: read the per-part report. Usually one part still carries an enzyme site, or two overhangs are the same.
  • A product is the right size but wrong inside: open it and look at the features. For a recombination, check which product you saved — the by-product is listed too.
  • Edited the wrong plasmid: open History and restore the version before your change.
  • Reads will not align: check the plasmid is marked circular if it is, and that you chose the right reference.
  • An enzyme is missing from the list: the chosen enzyme set may be a short one. Use Choose… to widen it.
  • Confirmed the wrong clone: confirm again with the right clone; the glycerol stock already made can be corrected or marked used up in Lab Stocks.
